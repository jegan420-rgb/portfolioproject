COLOR THEORY OVERVIEW

Color theory is a broad term for the collective principles and guidelines that define the use of color in design (and art, more generally). Further defined, it is the structural understanding and implementation of color. Essentially, it is the art and science of how colors interact: how they can combine to create harmony, how specific colors can complement one another, or conversely contrast with each other for effect.

Effective use of color can influence the thinking and feeling of the viewer. Color’s ability to influence or evoke emotions means a firm grounding in color theory is necessary across a number of disciplines, not limited to communication, branding and marketing, art, and design in its many forms.

Key Terms in Color Theory

  1. Color Wheel: A circular diagram that maps out the relationships between colors. Invented by Sir Isaac Newton in 1666, the color wheel is an essential tool for understanding and implementing color relationships.
  2. Primary Colors: Red, Green, and Blue. All of which are considered pure colors and cannot be created by mixing other colors.
  3. Secondary Colors: These colors are the result of mixing any two primary colors. Secondary colors include Green, Violet, and Orange.
  4. Tertiary Colors: These are the result of mixing a primary color with a secondary color. There are six tertiary colors in total: blue-violet, red-violet, blue-green, yellow-green, yellow-orange and red-orange.
  5. Complementary Colors: A color that, when combined with a given color, creates a grayscale color like white or black. When used together, complementary colors both contrast with and reinforce each other.
  6. Analogous Colors: These “color neighbors” are groups of three to five colors positioned adjacent to each other on the color wheel.
  7. Hue: A hue is a pure expression of a color, without white, black, or gray added.
  8. Shade: A shade is a hue mixed with black.
  9. Tint: A tint is a hue mixed with white.
  10. Tone: A tone is a hue mixed with gray.
  11. Warm Colors: Reds, oranges, and yellows are colors associated with heat and sunlight, hence “warm” colors, and can evoke a cozy or comfortable feeling.
  12. Cool Colors: Blues, greens, and purples are cool colors which are associated with water and sky, ice and snow. Cool colors evoke feelings of calm and are conducive to creating a sense or mood of relaxation.
  13. Neutral Colors: These are hues that lack saturation or intensity. Black, white, gray, beige, taupe, and brown are examples of neutral colors.
  14. Monochromatic: A monochromatic color scheme uses variations of a given color to create a unified aesthetic.
  15. Color Harmony: Aesthetically pleasing color combinations impact and influence the viewer by presenting a unified, cohesive and often compelling appearance.
  16. Contrast: The difference between different color hues is color contrast. Essentially, color contrast is how a given color stands out from another color and is an important consideration in many designs.
  17. Saturation: The intensity and purity of a hue is saturation. At 100% saturation the hue is its purest color expression, often quite vivid. At 0% saturation, that same color is reduced to a neutral gray color.
  18. Color Scheme: Established using the Color Wheel, a color scheme is a well-thought-out and methodical plan to combine specific colors to evoke intended emotions or moods and create visual harmony.
  19. Triadic Colors: A group of three colors that are evenly spaced on the color wheel are considered triadic colors. Such color schemes can be vibrant and create a sense of harmony, where no single color dominates.
  20. Key Color: A key color is a (or the) dominant color in a composition. It sets the overall mood of what is being created. When the term "key" is used in context of printing, it refers to black ink in the CMYK color model.
