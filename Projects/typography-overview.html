TYPOGRAPHY OVERVIEW

Typography can be defined as use of the proper typeface at the proper time. Typography can also be described as the style and appearance of printed or online material, and the process or technique of arranging, designing, or styling text.

Good typography makes written content more readable while also being visually compelling. Like color, typography can evoke emotions or set moods. Typography is vital in a number of areas, not limited to branding or brand identity and even UI design - where people actually interact with typefaces.

10 Key Terms in Typography

  1. Serif: The corners, feet, or similar flourishes at the ends of character strokes are serifs. These “feet” make the text more readable, particularly over long periods of time or when there’s a dense presentation of material. Serif fonts are often used in academic textbooks.
  2. Sans Serif: Simply stated, sans serif means without serifs. These typefaces lack the flourishes of serif typefaces, and offer a high degree of readability from a distance or in motion. Their appearance is typically clean and minimal and are well-suited for use on digital screens. Additionally, sans serif typefaces can be suitable for use in headings and titles, where the body of a given work is in a serif typeface.
  3. Typeface: A typeface is the overall design of a set of characters. Often confused with fonts, typefaces refer to a family of fonts, and so contain multiple fonts.
  4. Font: A font is a specific style and weight (stroke thickness) within a given typeface. A font is a specific use or iteration of the typeface it’s from.
  5. Kerning: The adjustment of space between specific letter pairs, which improves readability, is kerning.
  6. Tracking: This is uniform spacing throughout a single word, or a paragraph.
  7. Leading: This is the vertical space between lines of text.
  8. Baseline: The baseline is the invisible line on which letters sit, essential to the uniformity of text.
  9. X-Height: This is the height of lowercase letters (excluding ascenders and descenders) is the X-Height. Ascenders are the parts of lowercase letters that extend above the X-Height, while descenders are the parts of letters that extend below the baseline.
  10. Hierarchy: This is how text is organized to guide readers from start to finish, used to create a structure that’s clear and ordered. A typical hierarchy could be having a title or heading in a large, bold font. Subheadings, in a medium size font, can break up the body of the text and essentially categorize the work and make it easier to read. Then, the body text itself (which is typically most of the written content) should be a smaller sized font than subheadings. When all three are used together properly, the end result is more readable and visually appealing.
